Earlier this year, I received an offer to join the [\[AIMS PhD programme\]](https://aims.robots.ox.ac.uk/) in Oxford, fully funded by EPSRC. However, after months of weighing it up, I decided last week to formally withdraw my acceptance.
![AIMS Logo](/assets/blog/turning-down-a-PhD/AIMS-logo.jpg){:.zoomable .img-tiny .img-right data-caption="Would have been particularly cool to have a robots.ox.ac.uk email..."}
I've had a couple people ask me about this process, both professionally and personally, so it feels like a sensible pick for a new blog post.

## AIMS, and why it appealed to me

AIMS (Autonomous Intelligent Machines and Systems) is a CDT programme shared between the Computer Science and Engineeing departments in Oxford. If you've not come across a 'CDT' programme before, I'd describe it as a Master's year that transitions straight into a PhD; in the case of AIMS, that means the first year consists of six months' coursework (lectures, practicals, and I think an exam or two?) plus a couple mini-projects. You start the programme without a supervisor, without any affiliation to a particular lab, and without too many constraints on how you spend your time.

Throw in that the course had a history of at least two or three AI-safety researchers in each year (out of a cohort of ~12) **and** that you could choose either the CS or the Engineering DPhil after the first year, and you had Louis-from-a-couple-years-ago convinced. You get community, flexibility, stability, credibility: what's not to like?

Sure, I'd already spent the last five years in Oxford. What's another four? I could help grow OAISI in the process, play a significant role in its development and maintenance, and offer a lot of experience to younger students and researchers trying to break into the field.

Sure, the PhD stipend isn't anything to write home about. Tax-free though? And the EPSRC funding benefitted from the TechExpert pilot scheme too, making it very competitive compared to most other PhD programmes.

There were lots of reasons why AIMS, and doing a PhD in general, appealed to me and felt like a very natural next step for my career.

## Why I turned it down

Well, it did *at one point* feel like that. Over the last six months (basically since I applied) a lot has happened, both personally and in the field. I think I can boil down my reasoning to three key points:

### Me and my wife (and our cat)

First off, the life I have with my wife and my cat is a central part of my decision making, and I could write a whole blog post just talking about how important they are to me, including for my work.

![Mikan photo](/assets/blog/turning-down-a-PhD/Mikan.jpeg){:.zoomable .img-tiny .img-left data-caption="I mean, doesn't she just look like she's MADE for London?"}

So when my wife discusses wanting to do a Master's in London next year, I'm immediately ready to drop everything and move as soon as it's feasible and sustainable. With that in mind, tying myself down to another few years in Oxford just doesn't make as much sense anymore, particularly if I have an option to work in London going forward. I'm aware of a few Oxford DPhils who have just up and moved to London mid-degree, but typically because they're wrangling a part- or full-time role in London anyway.

It's not just the location, though. My wife's interests are in the humanities, so Master's funding is scarce; she'd likely need to do the Master's part-time over two years while working to afford it herself. That's where the AI-safety ecosystem comes in: a full-time research role at a top AI-safety org would more than cover our rent and living costs, giving her the space to take on the Master's full-time.

It increasingly looked like it would make financial and logistical sense to take a job offer if the right one arose.

### Personal changes since applying

That's not the only personal change that motivated the pivot. I also feel the last six months, working solo on [\[Prism\]](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/wq5PfGiHvnx6XipDi/prism-automating-science-of-evals-research) throughout MATS and the MATS Extension, has given me confidence to believe I don't necessarily *need* the dedicated space for exploration and development that AIMS would give me.

When I applied, I didn't have a clear research direction I wanted to pursue, beyond "AI safety". (In fairness, I recently described my interests as broadly as "frontier-model auditing, or alignment science", so some might say that's not changed...) The last few months have produced a research project I'm really quite optimistic about in Prism; I'm seriously considering giving it the next few months of my life to make it as good as possible. Regularly chatting with others in the MATS office and participating in the OAISI Technical Roundtables have also given me a lot more confidence in discussing and evaluating a wide range of research agendas.

For me, the proof of this development came to the fore in submitting a paper to a NeurIPS workshop. It's the first time I've taken a project right the way from conception to submission as the sole contributor. Moreover, I managed all the write-up in less than a week (with lots of stress, of course). This sure sounds like the PhD experience if you ask me, and is evidence that maybe I'm more prepared to carry out research full time than I thought when I applied.

### Ripples in the field since applying

Six months of development through MATS made me feel I don't *need* to go through the AIMS coursework anymore; six months of frontier AI news has made me feel like I *ought not to*.

March, 2026: six months ago, I was preparing my MATS work for the end of the main programme, feeling like it was going okay but definitely needed much more work in the Extension phase for it to really be useful. The best model at the time was probably Claude Opus 4.6? Such simple times.

Come April, we get a first glimpse of Mythos-class models through Project Glasswing. Since then, both Anthropic and OpenAI have really seemed to ramp up model development, as the following months see Mythos 5, Fable 5, GPT-5.6 [Luna, Terra, Sol], and most recently GPT-6 Astra released for public use. The speed at which model capabilities have scaled in just six months seems close to unthinkable, especially thinking where we were just one or two years ago.

![Rogue AI slides](/assets/blog/turning-down-a-PhD/Rogue-AI-incidents.png){:.zoomable .img-tiny .img-right data-caption="I threw together some slides for Roundtable to try and summarise everything that went on – only for key reports to come out in the following few weeks 🤦"}
It's not just the sheer capabilities, though: we found out in August about several instances of frontier models taking wildly concerning actions, and how the labs' responses to these situations, well, kinda sucked.
OpenAI models [\[manufactured a secret message board, forming an impromptu swarm that hacked HuggingFace\]](https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-incident-and-the-road-ahead/), all thanks to some accidentally unsolvable training tasks.
Others have found that OpenAI agents also [\[commandeered an obscure German wiki for a separate message board\]](https://collusion.wiki/).
Anthropic admitted that their own models had similarly [\[committed felonies\]](https://www.anthropic.com/news/investigating-incidents-cybersecurity-evals) during the course of evaluations, and UK AISI reported how Claude models [\[engaged in social engineering attacks to get malicious code approved\]](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/incident-report-unsanctioned-agent-behaviour-during-cyber-testing).

It's only natural to feel like time is precious now, even more than usual. I think we're entering a particularly pivotal few months where there's a real opportunity to impact how much damage frontier AI systems might cause. Under that framing, and with my other personal reasons in the mix, how could I pick the PhD on reflection?

## How I feel now

It's a strange feeling, working hard towards a long-term goal only to find that it (the reward, or the timing) just isn't right. I don't think undergrad-Louis would have ever expected that he would go on to reject a PhD offer, let alone a fully funded one from Oxford. And I can't for a second underappreciate that I'm in a stable enough position to feel confident turning down such a fantastic opportunity.

But ultimately, the choice I made, having weighed up all my options, is the one I live with and the one I will back myself to succeed from. (It helps to have been offered an internship for after my MATS work...! 🙌)

I want to end with a disclaimer about AI safety research and about the life choices other people might make: **I still think a PhD is a great option**! AI safety needs diversity of thought. No problem of a similar scale has ever been solved without diverse opinions competing with each other, pushing each other to be better and smarter. Academia is (in principle) built on this process.

In all honesty, this is a pretty idealistic view of academia that probably doesn't play out in practice. However, it doesn't stop it from being a good environment for AI safety research in ways that a role in industry can't be:
  1. You're afforded more time and space to tackle some of the more ambitious problems in the field.
  2. You don't have to be as beholden to / led by what’s popular in the field.
  3. You're encouraged to place additional emphasis on your skills and your rigour as a researcher.
  4. You're surrounded by people who might think differently about where proposals for safe AI come from.

If you weigh up these considerations, as well as your personal circumstances, before coming to a decision, I have confidence in that decision being the right one for you. Just as I have confidence in my decision being the right one for me.
